1. What Is CSET Physical Education Subtest I (129)?

CSET Physical Education Subtest I (Test Code 129) is the first of three subtests required to demonstrate subject matter competency for a Single Subject Teaching Credential in Physical Education in California. It is administered by Pearson on behalf of the California Commission on Teacher Credentialing (CTC).

Subtest I focuses on the foundational science behind movement and motor development — how people grow, learn, and refine physical skills, and how the body's systems function during exercise and activity. It is more academic than athletic: the exam tests your understanding of kinesiology, physiology, and motor learning theory, not your coaching résumé or playing experience.

You must pass all three subtests (129, 130, 131) to complete the CSET Physical Education subject matter requirement through examination. Each subtest is scored independently, and candidates may register for one subtest, two, or all three in a single test session.

2. Exam Structure

Subtest I consists of 40 multiple-choice questions and 2 short (focused) constructed-response questions, administered over 1 hour and 45 minutes at a Pearson test center. The exam is computer-based only.

Question TypeNumberFormat
Multiple Choice 40 questions 20 questions per domain, four options each
Constructed Response 2 questions (short/focused) One per domain, typed directly into the testing interface; one includes a videotaped component

Multiple-choice and constructed-response sections are weighted 70% and 30% respectively toward your overall scaled score. A distinctive feature of Subtest I (and Subtest III) is the videotaped constructed-response component: you'll watch a brief video excerpt — typically showing a movement, skill, or teaching scenario — and then write a response analyzing or applying course content to what you saw.

Subtest I is offered only as a computer-based test at an in-person Pearson test center. Unlike Subtest II, it is not available through online proctoring. Candidates can register to take Subtest I alone, or combine it with Subtests II and III in a single 5-hour session.

Registration & Fees

  • $99 to take Subtest I on its own
  • $297 to take all three subtests (129, 130, 131) together
  • Testing is offered by appointment during scheduled testing windows throughout the year
  • Results are typically available within a few weeks of your test date — check ctcexams.nesinc.com for the current release schedule

3. Topics Covered

Subtest I is built from two content domains, each contributing 20 multiple-choice questions and one constructed-response question. Understanding this breakdown is the fastest way to plan your study time efficiently.

Domain 1 — Growth, Motor Development, and Motor Learning

This domain tests how people acquire and refine movement skills across the lifespan, and the factors that help or hinder that process.

Topic AreaWhat You Need to Know
Individual Differences How genetics, body type, prior experience, and disability affect motor skill development between students
Perceptual-Motor Development How sensory input (vision, balance, proprioception) is processed and translated into coordinated movement
Physical and Developmental Changes Growth patterns across childhood and adolescence and how they affect motor performance and readiness
Motor Learning Stages of skill acquisition, types of practice (blocked, random, distributed), and feedback theory
Motor Task Analysis Breaking a movement skill into component parts to teach, assess, and progress it effectively
Conditions Affecting Growth, Motor Development, and Motor Learning Environmental, nutritional, and instructional factors that support or limit skill development
Developmental Differences Affecting Motor Skills Acquisition How age, maturation rate, and individual readiness change the pace at which students learn new skills

Domain 2 — The Science of Human Movement

This domain tests the scientific foundations of movement: how the body's systems work, how forces act on the body, and how exercise affects physical performance and health.

Topic AreaWhat You Need to Know
Body Systems How the musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, respiratory, and nervous systems function and interact during movement
Basic Kinematic and Kinetic Principles of Motion Speed, velocity, acceleration, force, and how these principles describe and explain movement
Biomechanical Principles Levers, torque, center of gravity, stability, and how mechanical principles apply to sport and movement skills
Movement Analysis Observing and breaking down a movement to identify errors and suggest corrections
Effects of Exercise Acute and chronic physiological responses to exercise across body systems
Components of Wellness Physical, mental, and social dimensions of wellness and how physical activity supports each
Physical Fitness Testing and Prescription Standard fitness assessments and how to design activity appropriate to test results and goals
Factors Affecting Physical Performance Nutrition, hydration, recovery, environment, and training variables that influence performance
Safety, Injury Prevention, and First Aid Common activity-related injuries, prevention strategies, and appropriate first-response actions
Physiological Principles of Fitness Overload, specificity, progression, and other core training principles behind fitness improvement

Important: Domain 2 (The Science of Human Movement) spans more individual topic areas than Domain 1, but both domains carry equal weight — 20 multiple-choice questions and one constructed-response question each. Don't let the longer topic list in Domain 2 crowd out your review time for Domain 1.

4. How Difficult Is the Exam?

Difficulty depends heavily on your academic and professional background. Here is an honest breakdown:

BackgroundLikely ExperienceTypical Prep Focus
Kinesiology or exercise science degree Body systems, biomechanics, and motor learning theory feel familiar; mainly a refresher Review terminology and practice writing focused constructed responses
Athletic training or coaching background Strong practical movement knowledge, but formal biomechanics and motor development theory may be new Build out Domain 2 science content and academic vocabulary
Classroom teacher, career changer Comfortable with instructional concepts, but both domains likely need direct study Work through both domains systematically, starting with core anatomy and physiology
Physical therapy, athletic training, or allied health background Strong anatomy and physiology foundation; motor development and pedagogy content may be less familiar Focus study time on Domain 1's motor learning and development theory

The exam rewards candidates who can connect scientific and developmental concepts to real classroom application — not just recall definitions. Candidates who struggle typically treat this as a coaching-knowledge test rather than the academic kinesiology exam it actually is.

5. Recommended Study Timeline

This 6-week plan works for most candidates with some prior coursework or professional experience in physical education or kinesiology. Compress it if you have a recent kinesiology degree; extend it if both domains are new to you.

Week 1

Diagnostic + Domain 1 Foundations

Take a diagnostic practice set to identify weak areas. Begin Domain 1: individual differences, perceptual-motor development, and physical/developmental changes across age groups.

Week 2

Motor Learning + Motor Task Analysis

Study stages of motor learning, practice and feedback theory, and how to break a movement skill into teachable components through task analysis.

Week 3

Domain 2 — Body Systems & Biomechanics

Review the musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, respiratory, and nervous systems, then move into kinematic, kinetic, and biomechanical principles of motion.

Week 4

Exercise Effects, Wellness & Fitness Testing

Cover the effects of exercise on the body, components of wellness, physical fitness testing, and the physiological principles behind fitness improvement.

Week 5

Constructed-Response Practice

Write one short constructed response each day, alternating between domains. Practice the videotaped-response format specifically — outline your answer while the clip is fresh, then write.

Week 6

Full Review + Timed Practice

Do a full timed run-through under exam conditions (1 hour 45 minutes). Review every missed concept, and spend your final days on your weaker domain.

6. Best Study Strategies

🎯
Study by domain, not by random topic list
Work through Domain 1 systematically, then Domain 2. Jumping between unrelated topics makes it harder to see how concepts connect.
🎥
Rehearse the videotaped-response format
Watch short clips of movement skills or PE instruction and practice writing a structured response quickly — this format is unique to this exam.
🏫
Tie every concept back to the K–12 classroom
The exam consistently rewards applying scientific and developmental concepts to real teaching scenarios, not just recalling definitions.
🧩
Build a body-systems and biomechanics glossary
Domain 2 leans on precise terminology. A running glossary of key terms makes multiple-choice questions far less ambiguous.

For constructed-response questions specifically

  • Keep responses short and focused — these are not extended essays, so lead with the key concept and support it directly
  • Use precise subject-matter vocabulary rather than casual or coaching language
  • For the videotaped item, note 2–3 specific observations from the clip before you start writing
  • Leave a minute or two to reread your response for clarity and completeness before moving on

7. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Common pitfalls candidates run into

  • Treating it like a coaching knowledge test — Subtest I is academic kinesiology and motor development science, not sport-specific trivia
  • Underestimating Domain 2 — biomechanics and physiology terminology can be unfamiliar to candidates without a science background
  • Skipping videotaped-response practice — the format is unusual if you've never tried it before test day
  • Confusing scaled score with percentage — 220 out of 300 is not 73%; don't set a percentage target
  • Studying too broadly — reviewing general exercise science instead of the specific topic areas within each domain
  • Not connecting theory to teaching — constructed responses score better when they apply concepts to a classroom scenario

9. Frequently Asked Questions

What does CSET Physical Education Subtest I (129) cover?
Subtest I covers two domains: Growth, Motor Development, and Motor Learning (individual differences, perceptual-motor development, motor learning theory, and motor task analysis) and The Science of Human Movement (body systems, biomechanics, effects of exercise, fitness testing, and injury prevention).
How many questions are on CSET PE Subtest I?
Subtest I has 40 multiple-choice questions and 2 short constructed-response questions, one from each domain. The session is 1 hour and 45 minutes and is computer-based only.
Is CSET PE Subtest I hard?
Difficulty depends on your background. Candidates with a kinesiology or exercise science degree usually find the material familiar. Candidates coming from coaching or teaching experience without formal science coursework often need to build up the biomechanics and physiology content in Domain 2 specifically.
Can I take CSET PE Subtest I online?
No. Subtest I is only available as a computer-based test at an in-person Pearson test center. Online proctoring is available for Subtest II, but not for Subtest I or Subtest III.
Do I need to take all three PE subtests at once?
No. You can register for one subtest, two, or all three in a single test session — but if you take all three together, plan for a full 5-hour session, since subtests are administered consecutively and you cannot return to a completed subtest.
What is the videotaped constructed-response component?
Subtest I (and Subtest III) include a constructed-response item built around a short video excerpt. You watch the clip and then write a response that applies subject matter knowledge to what you observed — practicing this format ahead of time is well worth the effort.
